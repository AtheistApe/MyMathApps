The setup

You have a first-order ODE

$$y'(t) = f(t, y), \qquad y(t_0) = y_0.$$

This says: at every point $(t, y)$, you know the slope of the solution curve. You also know one point the curve passes through — the initial condition. The question is: what is $y(t)$ for $t > t_0$?

For a few special $f$'s, you can solve this with calculus. For most $f$'s, you can't. So instead we approximate $y$ at a sequence of $t$-values

$$t_0, \quad t_1 = t_0 + h, \quad t_2 = t_0 + 2h, \quad \ldots, \quad t_n = t_0 + nh.$$

The number $h$ is the step size. At each $t_n$, we'll compute an approximation $y_n \approx y(t_n)$. Different methods differ in how they decide what $y_{n+1}$ should be, given everything they know up to step $n$.

That's the whole game. Every method below is just a different answer to that question.

Forward Euler order 1

Take the slope at where you are, and follow it for one step.

You're at the point $(t_n, y_n)$. The ODE tells you the slope right there is $f(t_n, y_n)$. So you draw a straight line with that slope, walk to $t_{n+1} = t_n + h$, and call where you ended up your new $y_{n+1}$.

$$y_{n+1} = y_n + h \cdot f(t_n, y_n).$$
Geometrically At each step, follow the tangent line to the solution curve at your current point. In the visualizer, the dashed red segment is exactly that tangent line. Forward Euler always lands on the tangent line, never on the actual curve.

Why it's not very accurate

The slope $f(t_n, y_n)$ is correct at the start of the step, but becomes wrong as soon as $y$ starts changing. Forward Euler keeps following the original slope all the way to $t_{n+1}$ — by the time it gets there, the real solution has curved away. Each step adds error proportional to $h^2$, and over $1/h$ steps that error accumulates to roughly $h$. So if you halve $h$, you halve the error.

That's what we mean when we call Forward Euler an "order 1" method.

Forward Euler is the simplest possible numerical method. Every other method on this page is some way of saying, "OK, but what if we were a little smarter about which slope to use?"

Heun's method order 2

Take a peek ahead, then average the slope at the start with the slope where the peek landed.

Forward Euler's flaw is that it commits to the slope at the start of the step. Heun's idea: don't commit yet. First, take a Forward Euler step — call where you'd land $\tilde y$. Now compute the slope there, at $(t_{n+1}, \tilde y)$. Average the two slopes, and use that average to take the actual step.

$$\begin{aligned} k_1 &= f(t_n, y_n) \quad \textit{(slope at the start)} \\ \tilde y &= y_n + h \cdot k_1 \quad \textit{(predictor: Forward-Euler peek)} \\ k_2 &= f(t_{n+1}, \tilde y) \quad \textit{(slope at the peek)} \\ y_{n+1} &= y_n + \tfrac{h}{2}(k_1 + k_2) \quad \textit{(corrector: averaged slopes)} \end{aligned}$$
Geometrically In the visualizer you'll see two things for each blue step:
  • A predictor leg — the dashed line Heun would have followed if it were just Forward Euler. It ends in a hollow circle at $(t_{n+1}, \tilde y)$.
  • A corrector slope tick at the predicted point — the slope $k_2$ Heun is about to incorporate.
The committed Heun segment uses the average of $k_1$ and $k_2$, so it lands somewhere between where Forward Euler would have landed and the true solution.

Why "averaging slopes" helps

Forward Euler systematically overshoots when the solution is curving away from its tangent line. Heun's trick is that the slope at the predictor point is also wrong — but wrong in roughly the opposite direction. Averaging them cancels most of the error.

The result: Heun's method has error per step proportional to $h^3$, and accumulated error proportional to $h^2$. Halving $h$ now reduces the error by a factor of 4.

RK4 order 4

Heun, but with four cleverly chosen slope evaluations.

If averaging two slopes gives you order 2, what does averaging four give you? Order 4 — provided the four slopes and their weights are chosen carefully. RK4 is the choice that has worked best in practice for nearly a century.

$$\begin{aligned} k_1 &= f(t_n, y_n) \\ k_2 &= f(t_n + \tfrac{h}{2}, y_n + \tfrac{h}{2} k_1) \\ k_3 &= f(t_n + \tfrac{h}{2}, y_n + \tfrac{h}{2} k_2) \\ k_4 &= f(t_n + h, y_n + h\, k_3) \\ y_{n+1} &= y_n + \tfrac{h}{6}(k_1 + 2k_2 + 2k_3 + k_4) \end{aligned}$$

Read the formula slowly:

  • $k_1$ is the slope at the start (just like Heun and Euler).
  • $k_2$ is the slope at the midpoint, estimated using $k_1$ to take a half-step.
  • $k_3$ is also the slope at the midpoint, but using $k_2$ to take that half-step instead. Two midpoint estimates, computed differently — they will not be exactly equal.
  • $k_4$ is the slope at the endpoint, estimated using $k_3$ to take the full step.

Then you weight the four slopes — the midpoint slopes count twice as much as the endpoint slopes — divide by 6, and that's your final slope. Multiply by $h$ to get the step.

Geometrically Four small × markers appear on the plot for each RK4 step, labeled $k_1$, $k_2$, $k_3$, $k_4$, at the points where each slope is sampled. Notice $k_2$ and $k_3$ are at slightly different $y$-values even though both are at the same $t$ — they're sampling the slope field from two different vertical positions.

Why does it work so well?

The weights $(1, 2, 2, 1)/6$ are chosen so that when you Taylor-expand the result and compare against the true solution, all the terms up to and including $h^4$ cancel out. The first surviving error term is order $h^5$ per step, $h^4$ accumulated.

That extra accuracy is huge. Halving $h$ in RK4 reduces error by a factor of 16. So a step size that gives you 1% error in Forward Euler gives you about 0.000005% error in RK4 — for only four times as much work per step.

Implicit Euler order 1

Use the slope at where you're going, not where you are.

Forward Euler uses $f(t_n, y_n)$ — the slope at the start of the step. Implicit Euler uses $f(t_{n+1}, y_{n+1})$ — the slope at the end:

$$y_{n+1} = y_n + h \cdot f(t_{n+1}, y_{n+1}).$$

But there's a catch: $y_{n+1}$ appears on both sides! You can't just compute it. You have to solve the equation

$$Y - y_n - h \cdot f(t_{n+1}, Y) = 0$$

for the unknown $Y = y_{n+1}$. This is what makes the method "implicit" — each step requires an inner solve.

Geometrically In the visualizer, each violet step shows a small vertical stack of hollow circles at $t_{n+1}$. Those are Newton's method iterates — the inner solver guessing values of $Y$ and refining its guess until it finds the value that satisfies the equation.

If it's the same order as Forward Euler, why bother?

Because of stability — it can take big steps on problems where Forward Euler explodes. We'll come back to this in "a word on stiffness" below.

Adams–Bashforth 4 order 4 · multistep

Use the slopes from the last four steps to extrapolate the next one.

Every method up to here has been a "single-step" method: $y_{n+1}$ is computed from $y_n$ alone, ignoring all the earlier nodes you've already worked out. That's a waste — you've evaluated $f$ at every previous node, and that information is just sitting there.

Adams–Bashforth uses it. AB4 fits a polynomial through the slopes at the last four nodes, and integrates that polynomial to predict the next $y$:

$$y_{n+1} = y_n + \frac{h}{24}\bigl(55 f_n - 59 f_{n-1} + 37 f_{n-2} - 9 f_{n-3}\bigr),$$

where $f_k = f(t_k, y_k)$. The funny weights $(55, -59, 37, -9)/24$ come from integrating a cubic polynomial — they are not arbitrary.

Geometrically Each amber step shows a thick translucent bracket joining the four nodes the formula uses. As the method advances, the bracket shifts forward by one node per step.

The chicken-and-egg problem

AB4 needs four prior nodes to start, but at the very beginning you only have $y_0$. The standard fix is to use RK4 to compute $y_1, y_2, y_3$, then switch to AB4 from there on. The visualizer marks bootstrap nodes as hollow squares instead of filled circles — those are the nodes RK4 produced before AB4 took over.

Why bother, given that RK4 already exists?

RK4 evaluates $f$ four times per step. AB4 evaluates $f$ once per step (using slopes already computed). For problems where evaluating $f$ is expensive — for example, when $f$ involves another simulation or a database lookup — AB4 can be roughly 4× faster than RK4 for the same accuracy.

Adams–Moulton 3 order 4 · predictor–corrector

Like AB4, but the formula includes the slope at the new point — so it's implicit, and we use AB to predict, then AM to correct.

Adams–Moulton is the implicit cousin of Adams–Bashforth. AM3 says

$$y_{n+1} = y_n + \frac{h}{24}\bigl(9 f_{n+1} + 19 f_n - 5 f_{n-1} + f_{n-2}\bigr).$$

Notice $f_{n+1} = f(t_{n+1}, y_{n+1})$ depends on the unknown $y_{n+1}$, just like Implicit Euler. We could solve for $y_{n+1}$ with Newton's method, but there's an even simpler approach for this case: predictor–corrector.

  1. Predict $y_{n+1}$ using AB3 (the explicit cousin): $$y^*_{n+1} = y_n + \frac{h}{12}\bigl(23 f_n - 16 f_{n-1} + 5 f_{n-2}\bigr).$$
  2. Evaluate the slope at the predicted point: $f^*_{n+1} = f(t_{n+1}, y^*_{n+1})$.
  3. Correct using the AM3 formula with $f^*_{n+1}$ in place of $f_{n+1}$.

This is the "PECE" pattern: Predict, Evaluate, Correct, Evaluate. Cheap, no nonlinear solver, and you keep most of the accuracy benefit of being implicit.

Geometrically Each teal step shows a hollow square at the predicted point $y^*_{n+1}$, and the committed solid circle is at the corrected point. The two are usually close, but not identical — the gap between them is exactly what the corrector is correcting.

A word on "order" and why it matters

When we say a method has "order $p$", we mean: when the step size is small enough, halving $h$ reduces the error by a factor of $2^p$.

Forward Euler   halve $h$ → error halves   (factor 2 = $2^1$)
Heun   halve $h$ → error reduces 4×   ($2^2$)
RK4   halve $h$ → error reduces 16×   ($2^4$)
AB4   halve $h$ → error reduces 16×   ($2^4$)
AM3   halve $h$ → error reduces 16×   ($2^4$)

This is exactly what the "Convergence study" button in the app verifies. Click it, and you'll see each method's empirical order computed from actual numerical experiments — they'll be very close to 1, 2, 4, 4, 4 (with some variation due to floating-point noise and pre-asymptotic behavior at large $h$).

The practical consequence: for a target error $\epsilon$, an order-1 method needs steps of size $h \sim \epsilon$, while an order-4 method only needs $h \sim \epsilon^{1/4}$. For $\epsilon = 10^{-6}$, that's $10^6$ Forward Euler steps versus $\sim 32$ RK4 steps. Even though each RK4 step does 4× the work of an Euler step, RK4 still wins by orders of magnitude.

A word on stiffness

Some ODEs are easy. Others are stiff — meaning they have one or more very fast-decaying components. The classic example is

$$y' = -50\, y, \qquad y(0) = 1,$$

whose exact solution is $y(t) = e^{-50t}$ — it crashes from 1 to nearly 0 in a fraction of a second.

Try Forward Euler on this with $h = 0.05$. The recurrence is

$$y_{n+1} = y_n + 0.05 \cdot (-50\, y_n) = y_n \cdot (1 - 2.5) = -1.5\, y_n.$$

Each step flips the sign and multiplies the magnitude by 1.5. Starting from $y_0 = 1$, you get $1, -1.5, 2.25, -3.375, \ldots$. The numerical solution oscillates and explodes, while the true solution decays smoothly to zero. That's instability.

Try Implicit Euler on the same problem. Its recurrence is

$$y_{n+1} = y_n + 0.05 \cdot (-50\, y_{n+1}) \implies y_{n+1} = \frac{y_n}{1 + 2.5} = \frac{y_n}{3.5}.$$

Each step divides by 3.5. The numerical solution decays smoothly. Stable, even though $h$ is large.

This is the whole reason implicit methods exist. They are slower per step (Newton's method inside each step is not free), but they remain stable at step sizes that would blow up an explicit method. For stiff problems, that's the difference between getting an answer and not.
