Overview

The Power Series Explorer visualizes how a power series defines a function. Instead of plotting a function all at once, it builds the graph point by point: you choose an \(x\)-value, and the app shows each partial sum converging to the function's value at that point. The result is an intuitive picture of convergence, interval of convergence, and the relationship between a series and the function it represents.

The Series Form

The app evaluates series of the general form:

$$\sum_{k=k_0}^{\infty} b(k)\,(x - c)^{ak+l}$$

where:

  • \(b(k)\) is the coefficient formula — any expression in \(k\) using standard math functions
  • \(a\) is the exponent multiplier (controls the power spacing: 1 for every power, 2 for even/odd powers)
  • \(l\) is the exponent shift
  • \(c\) is the center of the series
  • \(k_0\) is the starting index
Example: The Maclaurin series for \(\sin x\) has \(b(k) = \frac{(-1)^k}{(2k+1)!}\), \(a = 2\), \(l = 1\), \(c = 0\), and \(k_0 = 0\), giving \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^k}{(2k+1)!}\,x^{2k+1}\).

The b(k) field accepts JavaScript expressions. Available functions:

FunctionDescriptionExample
factorial(n)\(n!\)factorial(k) → \(k!\)
C(n, r)Binomial coefficient \(\binom{n}{r}\)C(2*k, k)
pow(a, b)\(a^b\)pow(-1, k) → \((-1)^k\)

You can also use ** for exponentiation: (-1)**k is equivalent to pow(-1, k).

Clicking & Touch

The core interaction:

  • Mouse (desktop): Click anywhere on the plot to start animating partial sums at that \(x\)-coordinate.
  • Apple Pencil / touch (iPad): Touch down to see coordinates, drag to find the exact point you want, then lift to start the animation.

During the animation:

  • Yellow dot — the current partial sum \(S_n(x)\), jumping to each successive value as terms are added.
  • Cyan dot — the Aitken-accelerated estimate (when enabled). See below.
  • Red dot — placed when the partial sums converge (successive values within one pixel of each other). This is the app's estimate of \(f(x)\).

Click additional points to build up the graph of \(f\) one red dot at a time.

Batch Animation (Dance)

Enter a number (1–200) and click Dance! to animate many points simultaneously. The app:

  1. Detects the interval of convergence within the visible \(x\)-range
  2. Picks that many random \(x\)-values inside the interval
  3. Advances all their partial sums in parallel, one term per step

Points near the center of convergence lock in (turn red) first, while points near the boundary keep dancing longer — a vivid illustration of how convergence speed varies across the interval.

Try the 1/(1−x) preset with 50 points and a 60 ms delay. Points near \(x = 0\) converge almost instantly; points near \(x = \pm 0.99\) keep bouncing for hundreds of steps.

Presets & Saving

Built-in presets are defined in presets.js. Each one sets the formula, parameters, and axis ranges. To add your own permanently, edit that file and redeploy.

My Saved Series lets you save and reload custom series configurations from the browser. These are stored in your browser's local storage, so they persist between sessions but are specific to each browser/device.

Saved series are per-browser. A series saved on your laptop won't appear on your iPad. For series you need everywhere, add them to presets.js.

Aitken's Δ² Acceleration

Some series converge painfully slowly. The geometric series \(\sum x^k\) at \(x = -0.998\), for example, needs thousands of terms before the partial sums settle down. Aitken's Δ² method is a technique for estimating where a sequence is heading, long before it gets there.

The Core Idea

Suppose you have a sequence of partial sums \(S_0, S_1, S_2, \ldots\) converging to some limit \(L\). At each step, the "error" is \(S_n - L\). For many sequences, consecutive errors have an approximately constant ratio:

$$\frac{S_{n+1} - L}{S_n - L} \approx r \quad \text{for some } |r| < 1$$

This is called linear convergence — each step removes a fixed fraction of the remaining error. The geometric series is the prototype: the errors literally form a geometric sequence with ratio \(r = x\).

Aitken's insight: if we know the errors behave geometrically, we can solve for \(L\) using just three consecutive partial sums. We don't need to know \(r\) in advance — it cancels out.

Deriving the Formula

Start with the assumption that the errors decay geometrically:

$$S_n - L \approx C \cdot r^n$$

for some constants \(C\) and \(r\). Writing this for three consecutive terms:

$$S_n - L = C r^n, \quad S_{n+1} - L = C r^{n+1}, \quad S_{n+2} - L = C r^{n+2}$$

Take the ratio of consecutive errors:

$$\frac{S_{n+1} - L}{S_n - L} = r = \frac{S_{n+2} - L}{S_{n+1} - L}$$

Cross-multiply:

$$(S_{n+1} - L)^2 = (S_n - L)(S_{n+2} - L)$$

Expand both sides and solve for \(L\). After some algebra, you get:

$$\boxed{\hat{S}_n = S_n - \frac{(\Delta S_n)^2}{\Delta^2 S_n}}$$

where \(\Delta S_n = S_{n+1} - S_n\) and \(\Delta^2 S_n = S_{n+2} - 2S_{n+1} + S_n\)

This is equivalent to:

$$\hat{S}_n = S_n - \frac{(S_{n+1} - S_n)^2}{S_{n+2} - 2 S_{n+1} + S_n}$$

The quantity \(\hat{S}_n\) is the Aitken estimate — our best guess for \(L\) using only \(S_n, S_{n+1}, S_{n+2}\).

The name "Δ²" comes from the denominator \(\Delta^2 S_n\), which is the second forward difference of the sequence. It measures how the rate of change itself is changing — analogous to the second derivative.

Worked Example

Example: Geometric series at x = −0.998

Consider \(\displaystyle \frac{1}{1-x} = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} x^k\) evaluated at \(x = -0.998\).

The true value is \(\frac{1}{1-(-0.998)} = \frac{1}{1.998} \approx 0.50050\).

The first few partial sums are:

$$S_0 = 1, \quad S_1 = 1 + (-0.998) = 0.002, \quad S_2 = 0.002 + 0.996004 = 0.998004$$

Already we see the problem: the partial sums are bouncing between near 0 and near 1, converging extremely slowly toward 0.50050.

Now apply Aitken's formula with \(S_0 = 1\), \(S_1 = 0.002\), \(S_2 = 0.998004\):

$$\Delta S_0 = S_1 - S_0 = 0.002 - 1 = -0.998$$ $$\Delta^2 S_0 = S_2 - 2S_1 + S_0 = 0.998004 - 0.004 + 1 = 1.994004$$ $$\hat{S}_0 = 1 - \frac{(-0.998)^2}{1.994004} = 1 - \frac{0.996004}{1.994004} \approx 1 - 0.49950 = 0.50050$$

From just three partial sums that ranged from 0.002 to 1.0, Aitken's formula extracted the limit 0.50050 — accurate to five decimal places!

Why does it work so well here? Because the geometric series has exactly geometrically decaying errors — the assumption behind Aitken's method is perfectly satisfied. For other series, the errors are only approximately geometric, so Aitken improves convergence but doesn't nail the limit in one shot. Still, the acceleration can be dramatic.

How the App Uses Aitken

When you check Aitken Δ² acceleration:

  • After the first three partial sums are computed, the app calculates \(\hat{S}_n\) at each step
  • The yellow dot still shows the raw partial sum — so you can see how slowly it's converging
  • A cyan dot shows the Aitken estimate — typically much closer to the limit
  • The convergence check uses consecutive Aitken estimates: when \(|\hat{S}_n - \hat{S}_{n-1}|\) is less than one pixel, the app places the red dot at the Aitken value
  • The partial sum overlay shows both values: \(S_n(x)\) in gold and \(\hat{S}(x)\) in cyan

When It Doesn't Work

Aitken's method has an important limitation that the app enforces:

Aitken cannot make a divergent series converge. If the terms \(b(k)(x-c)^{ak+l}\) are not going to zero, the series diverges — period. No extrapolation trick changes that.

Mathematically, Aitken's formula can produce a finite answer even for divergent series (for the geometric series at \(x = 2\), it gives \(-1\), which is the "analytic continuation" \(\frac{1}{1-2}\)). But this is not convergence of the series — it's a coincidental algebraic artifact.

The app guards against this: it checks that the absolute values of the terms are actually decreasing before accepting an Aitken-based convergence. If \(|b(k)(x-c)^{ak+l}|\) is growing, the nth-term test for divergence fails, and the app reports "max terms reached" regardless of what Aitken says.

Aitken also works best on linearly converging sequences. For sequences that converge faster (like Newton's method, which converges quadratically), the geometric-decay assumption is poor and Aitken may not improve things. For sequences that converge slower than geometric (logarithmic convergence), Aitken helps but doesn't perform miracles.

Classroom Tips

  • Start simple. Load the \(e^x\) preset and click a few points. It converges fast everywhere — good for showing the basic point-by-point idea without long waits.
  • Show the interval of convergence. Switch to \(1/(1-x)\) and click at \(x = 0.5\) (converges quickly), then \(x = 0.9\) (slower), then \(x = 1.1\) (diverges). The contrast is immediate.
  • Use Dance! for the big picture. 50 points at 60 ms makes the shape of the function emerge in a wave from center outward. Toggle "Reveal full graph" afterward to confirm the points landed on the curve.
  • Demonstrate Aitken. Use \(1/(1-x)\) at \(x = -0.998\). First show it without Aitken — the yellow dot bounces between 0 and 1 for hundreds of steps. Then check Aitken and repeat — the cyan dot snaps to ~0.5 almost immediately while the yellow dot keeps bouncing.
  • Collapse the sidebar (click ◂) before projecting to give the plot maximum screen space.
  • Slow the animation to 400–600 ms when explaining what partial sums are. Speed it up to 20–60 ms for building the graph quickly.
