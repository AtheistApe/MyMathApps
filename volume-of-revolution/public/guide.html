Volume of Revolution Explorer

A guide to using the disk/washer & cylindrical shell app

Slice a solid one way and you get disks. Slice it the other way and you get shells. Both give the same volume.

What this app is for

You give it a curve — or two — and an axis to spin it around. It builds the resulting 3D solid two different ways: as a stack of disks/washers, and as a set of nested cylindrical shells. You can watch each method's approximation converge to the same exact volume as you add more pieces, pull out and inspect any single piece, and see both methods' numbers side by side to confirm they really do agree.

The idea in one picture

Take the region between a curve and the x-axis (or between two curves) and spin it around a line. That produces one 3D solid. There are two standard ways to break that solid into small pieces you can add up:

  • Disks / washers — thin slices perpendicular to the axis, stacked along it like coins.
  • Cylindrical shells — thin tubes parallel to the axis, nested inside each other like tree rings.

They look completely different, but they're describing the same object — so their volumes must match. That agreement is the main thing this app is built to show you.

Setting up a region

The Region panel on the left defines what gets rotated.

f(x)

The main curve. Type any expression in x — x^2, sqrt(x), sin(x) + 2, and so on. Standard math notation works; ^ is exponent, and you can write things like 2x^2 without needing a *.

g(x)

The second boundary curve. Leave it at 0 and the region is just "under f(x), above the x-axis" — the usual single-curve setup. Give it a real function and the region becomes the area between the two curves instead.

presets

Choosing an axis

The Axis of revolution panel picks the line the region spins around. This is the one choice that determines which solid you end up with — everything else is just how you look at it.

Horizontal / Vertical

Horizontal spins the region around a line y = k. Vertical spins it around a line x = k.

k

Where that line sits. k = 0 puts the axis right on the x-axis (or y-axis, for a vertical axis). Move it away from the region and you'll usually get a washer (a disk with a hole) instead of a solid disk, since there's now a gap between the axis and the nearer edge of the region.

If the axis line actually cuts through the middle of your region, the app will warn you that the solid may self-intersect. It still computes something, but it's no longer a straightforward "spin this region around a line outside it" problem — move k so the axis sits outside the region to get back to a standard setup.

Picking a method

The Method panel is separate from the axis on purpose — for a given axis, either method can describe the resulting solid. Switching between them doesn't change the shape you're rotating; it only changes how the app slices it up and displays it.

Disks / Washers

Shows the solid as a stack of flat, coin-like slices.

Cylindrical Shells

Shows the same solid as a set of nested tube-shaped slices.

The Volume panel below the 3D view always shows both methods' numbers at once, regardless of which one is currently displayed — that's where you can directly confirm they agree.

Controlling the approximation

Subintervals: n

How many pieces the solid is chopped into, from 1 to 40. This is the Riemann-sum knob: small n gives a rough, blocky approximation you can see the individual pieces of; large n gives something that looks smooth and whose volume sits very close to the exact answer.

Sample point

left, midpoint, or right — which point in each subinterval the app measures the curve at to size that piece. This is the same left/midpoint/right choice you'd make setting up any Riemann sum.

Show exact solid (ghost)

Overlays a faint, high-resolution version of the true solid behind your n-piece approximation, so you can see how closely the blocky version hugs the real shape.

Inspecting one piece

The Inspect a single piece panel lets you pull out and examine exactly one disk or shell.

Piece slider

Drag to select any piece by number, or click directly on a piece in the 3D view — either way selects it and lights it up amber.

Clear selection

Deselects the current piece and returns to the normal view.

Hide other pieces

Isolates the selected piece by hiding everything else, useful when a piece is small or tucked behind others.

Once a piece is selected, the Selected piece panel on the right shows its exact dimensions — radius, thickness, height, whatever's relevant to the current method — and the volume formula for that one piece worked out with real numbers.

Reading the 3D view

Drag to rotate the view, scroll to zoom, and click any piece to select it. Everything in the scene follows one consistent color language:

  • Teal — the disk or shell pieces making up the approximation
  • Amber — the selected piece, and the dashed axis of revolution
  • Violet — the faint exact solid, when "Show exact solid" is on

The white curve traces f(x); a lighter dashed line traces g(x).

Reading the numbers

Generating region

The small 2D panel under the 3D view shows the flat region before it's spun — the curve(s), the axis (dashed amber), and a highlighted rectangle or band showing exactly which slice of the region the currently selected piece came from.

Volume — two methods, one solid

This is the results panel: approximate and exact volume for Disk/Washer, then the same two numbers for Shell. When the exact values agree to within about 1%, you'll see a green confirmation — that's the "yes, these really are the same solid" moment. If they don't agree, it's almost always because the axis is cutting through the region (see the warning above).

Glossary

Axis of revolution
The line a 2D region is spun around to create a 3D solid.
Disk
A solid circular slice — used when the region touches the axis directly, so there's no hole.
Washer
A disk with a hole in the middle — used when there's a gap between the axis and the nearer edge of the region.
Cylindrical shell
A thin, hollow tube — the building block of the shell method, nested at increasing radius from the axis.
Riemann sum
An approximation built by adding up many small pieces; here, the sum of disk, washer, or shell volumes for a given n.
